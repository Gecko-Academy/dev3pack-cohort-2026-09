# Demonstrating, and defending [[defending]]

## The difference

A demonstration shows that something can work. A defence shows that you know
when it does not, and what happens then. Only the second is evidence, because
the first is satisfied by one lucky run.

Every session in this course closed the same way: something worked, then it was
broken on purpose, and the interesting part was what the system did next. Demo
day is that shape one more time, in front of people.

## What a defence sounds like

Weak, and common:

> It handles unsupported questions well. Let me show you.

Better, and what the rubric rewards:

> It refuses when retrieval returns nothing above the score floor. Here is the
> trace for that. It does **not** catch a document that is retrieved, cited,
> and does not actually support the claim: I found that in session 7 with the
> cite-everything test, and it is the top item on my list.

The second answer is shorter on confidence and much longer on information. It
names a mechanism, points at evidence, and states a known limit before anybody
has to find it.

## The three questions you will be asked

Whatever the demo, the room converges on these. Have the answer ready with a
file or a number attached.

1. **How do you know?** Not "it seemed right" — the evaluation report, the hit
   rate, the trace.
2. **What would change your mind?** The measurement that would reverse your
   architecture decision. If nothing would, it was a preference, not a decision.
3. **What breaks it?** You already know one thing. Say it before the failure
   you drew makes it obvious.
